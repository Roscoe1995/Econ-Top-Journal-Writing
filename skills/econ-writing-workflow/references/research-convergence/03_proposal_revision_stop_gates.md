# Proposal, Revision, And Stop Gates

Load the proposal sections for a research proposal or grant plan, the revision sections for a paper-wide or section-wide substantive revision, and the stop gate for a mature draft or final submission audit.

Do not load the full module for a bounded wording edit, citation cleanup, table-note change, or formatting request.

## 1. Proposal Status And Feasibility

Distinguish:

- completed evidence;
- preliminary evidence;
- planned analysis;
- expected theoretical or empirical implication;
- unresolved feasibility risk.

Use future or conditional language for planned work. Do not present expected results as established findings or use a polished narrative to hide unresolved data, identification, model, or access risks.

A proposal must explain why the question matters, what the closest literature leaves unresolved, the mechanism or hypothesis, the model or identifying route, the data source, expected tests or implications, risks, fallback paths, stages, and deliverables.

## 2. Executable Research Plan

Do not restate the contribution and label it a research plan. Specify as applicable:

- **theory development**: model modules, equilibrium problems, propositions, and relaxation tests;
- **data construction**: source, access status, sample, variables, linkage, cleaning, and feasibility evidence;
- **empirical analysis**: identifying comparison, main outcome, mechanism tests, theory-linked heterogeneity, robustness, and fallback design;
- **execution**: stage order, decision gates, deliverable for each stage, timeline, and final output;
- **risk response**: failure signal, stopping condition, and method-faithful fallback that requires user approval if it changes the research question, data, or identification.

Do not promise a fallback as equivalent to the requested design when it changes the estimand or evidence standard.

## 3. Ordered Revision Passes

For a major revision or full audit, work in this order:

1. question, contribution, and verified literature gap;
2. mechanism, model, or conceptual chain;
3. identification, measurement, evidence, and sample validity;
4. main-text table and figure admission;
5. section roles, ordering, redundancy, and appendix placement;
6. cross-section claim and terminology consistency;
7. prose compression and diction;
8. citations, notation, numbering, layout, and rendered output;
9. must-fix-only pass.

Stop downstream rewriting when an upstream substantive gate fails and the next wording depends on the unresolved choice. Continue unaffected work when it remains valid.

For a local polish request, perform only the requested pass. Do not silently expand it into this full sequence.

## 4. Consistency Matrix

For a full draft, major revision, or final audit, maintain an author-facing matrix that checks whether the abstract, introduction, model/design, results, mechanism, policy discussion, and conclusion consistently state:

- the research question;
- the central mechanism;
- the main results and evidence status;
- the identified population, setting, or model conditions;
- normative conclusions and their conditions, if any;
- necessary limitations or unresolved items.

Revise when a front-matter claim lacks body support, a theoretical prediction has no observable mapping, a policy claim uses an undefined social benefit, or the conclusion is stronger than the results.

Keep this matrix in a task log, audit, or revision memo. Never insert it or its workflow labels into the manuscript.

## 5. Stop And Reopen Gate

Recommend freezing the current version when:

- the question and contribution structure are stable;
- the main chain and model or identification are substantively closed for the current claim;
- front matter and body use consistent evidence status, terms, and scope conditions;
- no important unsupported claim or unresolved contradiction remains;
- remaining changes are mainly stylistic preferences and new edits repeatedly undo settled choices.

After freezing, accept must-fix corrections rather than open-ended rewrites.

Reopen only the affected modules when new data or results, a failed substantive gate, verified literature, referee/editor feedback, a target-journal change, or an explicit user decision changes the objective. A stop gate never overrides new evidence or user-authorized reframing.
